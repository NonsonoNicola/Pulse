## Early signs
Thirst, dark urine, reduced urine output, headache, fatigue, dizziness on standing, higher resting heart rate, lower energy. In microgravity, fluid shifts can make the usual signs less obvious.

## Typical evolution
Mild dehydration reduces performance and increases heart rate. Progressive dehydration can cause confusion, fainting, and kidney stress. Vomiting or diarrhoea can accelerate fluid loss.

## Environmental factors
Cabin temperature and humidity, exercise, EVA suit time, illness, diuretics, and limited access to fluids during operations.

## What to check (tests and measures)
Fluid intake and urine colour, body weight trend, heart rate and blood pressure, orthostatic symptoms, and vomiting or diarrhoea. Use the urine test strips and scale when available.

## First actions
Drink fluids in small frequent amounts. Use oral rehydration salts if losses are significant. Rest and monitor urine output. Contact the CMO if unable to keep fluids down.

## Escalate when
Confusion, fainting, very low urine output, persistent vomiting, severe weakness, or signs of shock. Escalate early when the link delay is long.

## Do not
Do not give large rapid fluid loads without CMO direction. Do not rely on thirst alone. Do not ignore weight loss or dark urine in a crew member who feels unwell.

## Plain-language summary (for non-medical crew)
Dark urine and feeling tired or dizzy can mean you need fluids. Drink small amounts often, use rehydration salts if you have been vomiting, and tell the CMO if you cannot keep fluids down.

## Required resources (inventory)
Oral rehydration salts, water, urine test strips, scale, blood pressure cuff, IV fluids if directed by CMO.
