## Early signs
Rising temperature, higher resting heart rate, lower heart-rate variability, poor sleep, fatigue, sore throat, cough, abdominal symptoms, or a skin wound. Fever may be blunted in spaceflight and skin temperature is not core temperature.

## Typical evolution
Viral and bacterial infections can progress quickly in a confined crew. Early non-specific signs may precede focal symptoms. Watch for breathing difficulty, confusion, severe pain, rash, or inability to keep fluids down.

## Environmental factors
Close quarters, shared air and surfaces, immune changes in spaceflight, sleep loss, and radiation. A second crew member with similar symptoms raises concern for transmission.

## What to check (tests and measures)
Oral temperature, heart rate and blood pressure, oxygen saturation, hydration, respiratory rate, throat and lung exam if trained, and a symptom timeline. Use the onboard thermometer and a second device when readings conflict. Collect a targeted history of exposures.

## First actions
Rest, hydrate, measure oral temperature, isolate if a contagious illness is suspected, and contact the CMO. Follow the fever protocol and use only medications approved by the CMO.

## Escalate when
High or persistent fever, breathing difficulty, chest pain, confusion, fainting, low oxygen saturation, severe dehydration, or rapid deterioration. Open an SBAR packet for ground review when delay is significant.

## Do not
Do not start antibiotics without CMO direction. Do not give dose numbers in free text. Do not assume a normal skin temperature excludes fever. Do not ignore a second crew member becoming ill.

## Plain-language summary (for non-medical crew)
Fever plus a faster heartbeat and feeling unwell can be an infection. Measure your temperature, drink, and tell the CMO early. Do not take antibiotics on your own.

## Required resources (inventory)
Oral thermometer, blood pressure cuff, pulse oximeter, stethoscope, oral rehydration salts, medication cabinet, IV fluids if directed by CMO.
