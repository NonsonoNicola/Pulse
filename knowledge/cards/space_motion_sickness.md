## Early signs
Nausea, stomach awareness, dizziness, headache, sweating, and reduced appetite, especially after arrival in microgravity or a gravity transition.

## Typical evolution
Symptoms often improve over days as adaptation occurs. Vomiting can cause dehydration. Persistent vertigo or neurological signs need prompt evaluation.

## Environmental factors
Gravity transitions, head movement, visual-vestibular conflict, CO2, dehydration, and fatigue.

## What to check (tests and measures)
Hydration, temperature, blood pressure, neurological symptoms, and vomiting frequency. Ask about similar episodes and medications.

## First actions
Move slowly, avoid sudden head movements, stay hydrated, eat small bland meals, and rest. Contact the CMO if vomiting prevents fluid intake.

## Escalate when
Persistent vomiting, inability to drink, severe headache, confusion, weakness, vision change, chest pain, or symptoms that worsen after a gravity transition.

## Do not
Do not dismiss neurological symptoms as motion sickness. Do not give repeated antiemetics without CMO review.

## Plain-language summary (for non-medical crew)
Feeling sick after a gravity change is common. Move slowly and drink. If you keep vomiting or have weakness, confusion or vision changes, tell the CMO now.

## Required resources (inventory)
Oral rehydration salts, medication cabinet, blood pressure cuff.
