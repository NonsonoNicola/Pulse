## Early signs
A reading outside the plausible range, a flat signal, an impossible jump, or values that conflict with heart rate, breathing, symptoms, or a second sensor. Pulse oximetry is especially sensitive to motion, cold hands and skin pigmentation.

## Typical evolution
A single bad reading can trigger a false alarm. Systematic drift can bias the baseline. Cross-checks and quality flags prevent bad data from becoming trend evidence.

## Environmental factors
Sensor placement, movement, temperature, battery, electromagnetic interference, and calibration. The person's own report may be more reliable than a faulty device.

## What to check (tests and measures)
Repeat with a second device, check placement and battery, compare with heart rate and respiratory rate, and ask a neutral question about how the person feels. Do not challenge the person's honesty.

## First actions
Mark the reading as suspect, exclude it from the personal baseline, ask for a repeat measurement, and use an alternative device. If the pattern persists, replace the sensor.

## Escalate when
The repeat measurement confirms a concerning value, or the person has symptoms that fit the reading. Data-quality problems do not override red-flag symptoms.

## Do not
Do not accuse the astronaut of faking data. Do not turn sensor reliability into a personal score. Do not let a suspect reading lower an alert triggered by symptoms.

## Plain-language summary (for non-medical crew)
If a reading looks wrong, we check the device, not you. Repeat it with another sensor and tell us how you feel. Bad data never counts against you.

## Required resources (inventory)
Second pulse oximeter, thermometer, blood pressure cuff, device log.
