## Early signs
Shorter sleep duration, lower sleep efficiency, daytime fatigue, irritability, reduced motivation, slower reaction times. Wearable sleep estimates may disagree with how the crew member feels.

## Typical evolution
Sleep debt accumulates. Performance and mood decline before people notice. Heart-rate variability often falls and resting heart rate rises. Long-term disruption can affect immune and metabolic function.

## Environmental factors
Light exposure, noise, temperature, workload, schedule shifts, CO2, and psychological stress. Spacecraft sunrise every 90 minutes can disrupt circadian cues.

## What to check (tests and measures)
Sleep diary or wearable summary, sleep quality rating, caffeine timing, work schedule, light exposure, noise. Ask about snoring or breathing pauses if fatigue is unexplained.

## First actions
Protect a consistent sleep window, dim light before sleep, reduce late workload and caffeine, keep the cabin quiet and cool, and use scheduled rest rather than pushing through fatigue.

## Escalate when
Sleep remains poor for more than a week, daytime sleepiness interferes with safety-critical tasks, or there are breathing pauses, chest symptoms or severe mood changes.

## Do not
Do not frame sleep loss as a lack of discipline. Do not rely on a single wearable night. Do not recommend sedatives without CMO review.

## Plain-language summary (for non-medical crew)
Sleep is a safety system. If you are sleeping badly, say so early; the fix may be schedule, light, noise or air, not willpower.

## Required resources (inventory)
Wearable or sleep log, optional earplugs and eye mask.
